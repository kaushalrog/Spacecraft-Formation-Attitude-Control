Keeping four spacecraft pointed the same way when their actuators weaken

Team 14: C Rishitha, Y Karthikeya, Ashwin Tyagi and S Kaushal. School of Artificial Intelligence, Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham, Coimbatore.

In a formation mission, several spacecraft have to turn and hold the same attitude together. In practice actuators lose effectiveness, torque is limited, there are disturbances, and no model is exact. Updating every controller at every time step also costs communication and computation.

We rebuilt the approach from Xie, Sheng and Chen's 2024 paper in MATLAB and Simulink, and compared three ways of scheduling the same fault-tolerant controller.

Open the interactive simulation

Four spacecraft, their links and their updates. Switch cases, inject an actuator fault and watch the errors settle.

Three ways to schedule the controller

Same sliding-mode controller, same faults and limits. The only difference is when each spacecraft updates its command.

Case 1: every time step

The baseline. Commands update continuously, which costs the most communication and computation.

Case 2: dynamic event-triggered

A spacecraft updates only when its error has drifted far enough since the last update.

Case 3: self-triggered

Each spacecraft works out in advance when its next update is due, so it doesn't have to keep checking.

What we built

  • A nonlinear attitude model of four spacecraft using Modified Rodrigues Parameters, with 3 attitude and 3 angular-velocity states each, 24 in total.
  • A distributed sliding-mode controller. Each spacecraft uses its own error and its neighbours' errors through the communication graph.
  • Actuator effectiveness loss, time-varying disturbances, model uncertainty and torque limited to ±0.2 N·m.
  • The three triggering cases above, in one Simulink model with a case selector.

What we saw

All three cases bring every spacecraft to the shared attitude, and the angular-velocity errors settle too. The torque commands stay inside the ±0.2 N·m limit throughout.

The difference is how often the controller acts. In case 2 the torque switches back and forth more often, because the command only changes at trigger events, but it stays within the limit. The trigger plots show each spacecraft updating at its own irregular times instead of on a fixed clock. That's where the savings in communication and computation come from.

All 14 figures, Fig02 to Fig15, are in the output_images folder. Running sff_run_all in MATLAB regenerates them.

What this doesn't cover yet

  • Everything is numerical simulation. There's no hardware-in-the-loop or experimental test.
  • Only four spacecraft. We haven't measured how communication and computation grow with larger formations.
  • Faults are a fixed loss of actuator effectiveness. Multiple, time-varying or intermittent faults, communication delays and packet loss aren't modelled.

Next steps would be hardware-in-the-loop testing, larger formations, richer fault and communication models, and adaptive tuning of the controller gains and trigger parameters.
